What this tool does — and what it does not

✔ It does
  • Search 4,903 real, already-fired glaze recipes
  • Rank them by chemical similarity to your target colour
  • Give you the full recipe: oxides, weighed ingredients, cone, atmosphere, UMF
  • Report a calibrated uncertainty interval on every prediction
  • Show the colour actually measured for that recipe, next to its prediction
✘ It does not
  • Predict the exact colour a recipe will fire to in your kiln
  • Account for firing curve, soak, cooling rate or layer thickness
  • Replace a test tile
Why. Colour in this dataset is read from community photographs, and 82.7 % of its variance is within-chemistry — chemically identical recipes fire to colours about 48 sRGB units apart. The two candidate colours automatically extracted from each photo sit 150 units apart, and a human annotator cannot decide between them in 17.7 % of cases. A model error of ~38 units is therefore below the noise floor of its own target. Treat the output as a shortlist of real recipes worth testing, not a colour guarantee.

Honest limits. 82.7 % of colour variance in this dataset is within-chemistry variance: chemically identical recipes fire to colours differing by ~48 sRGB units. The ceiling for any chemistry-only model is ≈0.17–0.28, so these figures are at the information limit of the data — not a tuning failure. Predictions are therefore reported as calibrated intervals, never as exact values.

Both improvement levers were tested and both failed. Adding 41 physics-derived features (thermal expansion via six classical glass-science models, polymerisation index, chromophore loading) changed R² by −0.0000 (95 % CI contains zero) — because those quantities are already implicit in the raw oxides. Curating labels changed nothing either: four independent annotators agree 100 % of the time, so the labels are not the noise source. See the summary or the full study.
